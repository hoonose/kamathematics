TCS+ Receives the TCMF Distinguished Service Award

19 November 2026 · Gautam Kamath

TCS+ was recently recognized with the 2026 IEEE/Computer Society TCMF Distinguished Service Award. I served as a co-organizer of TCS+ from roughly 2014 to 2020. I and the whole team am honoured and grateful for the recognition by the committee.

TCS+ is an online seminar series that has been running continuously since 2013, hosting over 200 seminar-style talks. The purpose of the series has been to broaden access to high-quality talks in theoretical computer science, as traditionally, such content is only available to those at a small number of elite institutions in a restricted set of locales. Talks are streamed live and later posted on YouTube. Online seminars were relatively uncommon when TCS+ was founded, and it has helped and influenced several other online seminars and similar events.

I particularly appreciate the committee's decision to recognize TCS+ as an organization, which is not the norm for such awards. Indeed, most service recognitions are given to a single individual or a small group of individuals. In comparison, TCS+ has had 19 co-organizers over the years. Here's a couple reasons why I think this is a very thoughtful decision.

When it comes to research, the theoretical computer science community already realizes that there are different ways to highlight contributions. One way is to recognize a single individual, generally for work over the course of their career, via the Knuth Prize. Another way is to recognize a single research contribution (i.e., a paper), often conducted by a team, with the Gödel Prize. For service, we seem to focus almost entirely on the former "individual" category. There are great reasons to recognize such individuals! For example, many people with immense service contributions are great role models that the community should learn from. At the same time, there are excellent service contributions which take the form of an effort or an initiative. More often than not, these initiatives are due to the work of several people, whose specific contributions may vary. But, similar to the Gödel Prize, it makes sense to center recognition around the collective "thing" produced.

It also raises the question: if one tried to recognize individuals (rather than the initiative itself), how should one do it? None of the possible answers are satisfying. Given that individual contributions vary, equally recognizing all 19 co-organizers might be unfair. On the other end of the spectrum, exclusively awarding the three (visionary) co-founders (Anindya De, Oded Regev, and Thomas Vidick) would leave out the immense work of, e.g., Clément Canonne, who, having served continuously as a co-organizer since 2014, has the longest tenure with TCS+ by a large margin. The common sense solution is similar to how we recognize papers: celebrate the work done, and let people claim credit individually based on what they contributed.

No one does community service because they are hoping to win awards for it. But nonetheless it is heartening to see an institution that has long served our community be recognized. TCS+ owes its success and longevity not only to its diverse set of organizers, but to the entire community: if you ever joined us for a live talk, shared an announcement with a colleague, or watched a recording on YouTube, thank you for supporting TCS+.
